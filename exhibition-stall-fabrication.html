Exhibition Stall Fabrication: How a Stall Is Built and Why Fit Matters

Fabrication is the stage where a drawing meets gravity. This page shows how a stall goes from frame to finish, and why building in-house, and test-assembling where needed, prevents most on-site problems.

In short: We fabricate custom, modular and hybrid exhibition stalls in our own workshops in Karachi, Islamabad and Lahore, using materials chosen for each job and test-assembling stalls before they ship where the project calls for it.

The build sequence

  1. Frame: timber or aluminium structure sized to the drawing.
  2. Panels: boards and sheets cut and fitted to the frame.
  3. Finish: paint, laminates or surface treatments, and edge sealing.
  4. Graphics and fittings: printed panels, lettering, lighting and electrics.
  5. Test assembly (where the project calls for it): the stall is built once in the workshop and checked.
  6. Label and pack: parts are numbered so on-site assembly follows the order they were tested.

Materials are chosen per job, not per habit

We work with the material that suits the requirement: MDF and plywood for shaped, paintable walls and counters, aluminium for light, reusable frames, acrylic for clean displays and illuminated surfaces, laminates for durable colour, fabric for seamless graphics, and LED for lighting. The right choice depends on how often the stall is reused, how far it travels and your budget.

Custom, modular or hybrid?

A custom stall is built once to one design. A modular stall uses reusable frames and panels that can be reconfigured. A hybrid pairs a modular structure with custom elements such as a counter or feature wall. We build all three. See stall types and starting prices.

Where your stall is built

Rented stalls are mostly built on site inside the expo centre of each city. Custom products you buy outright are built in our workshops and, where your requirements call for it, test-assembled once before shipping so fit and finish are checked.

Why in-house changes the outcome

Design, fabrication and printing happen inside our own teams, with local workshops in three cities. A change to a dimension becomes a conversation with the person building it, not an email chain. Test assembly means fit problems appear in the workshop, where they are cheap to fix.
